There's a particular kind of embarrassment when someone asks, "did you get a chance to look at that thing?" and you have absolutely no idea what thing they mean.

You said you would. You meant it. And then it fell into the gap between a chat app, an email thread and your own head.

Most of what goes wrong between people isn't conflict. It's a dropped follow-up.

## Why people slip through the cracks

The details of our relationships are scattered everywhere. What we talked about is in one chat app. The plan is in an email. The date is in the calendar. What I promised is, at best, in my head.

None of those places knows about the others. So before I meet someone, I scroll. After I meet them, I tell myself I'll remember. And I usually don't.

## A people section, not a sales tool

Businesses have a whole category of software for this, called a CRM ("customer relationship manager"). Most of it is built for sales teams chasing deals, and it feels like it.

What I wanted was smaller: a section of my memory for the people in my life and work, connected to everything else. For each person:

- **Who they are** and how we know each other.
- **What we talked about last**, in a line or two.
- **What I promised them**, and by when.
- **What they promised me.**
- **Which plans or projects they're part of.**

That's it. No pipeline stages, no scores. Just enough that I never have to say "remind me what we decided?"

## Three moments where it pays off

Here's how it works on a normal week, with made-up examples from my demo notebook.

**Before I meet someone**, I ask:

> I'm meeting Alex at 3:30 about Mum's birthday. Prep me.

What comes back: what's already decided, what's still open, the gift-ideas note, what Alex offered to do last time, and three questions to settle today.

**After I meet them**, one message, the way I'd actually say it:

> Great catch-up with Alex. They'll book the restaurant by Friday. I said I'd sort out the cake.

The AI adds a note on Alex, a follow-up to check the restaurant on Friday, and a to-do for the cake under the birthday plan. All linked, all in the diary.

**In between**, my morning brief does the remembering:

> You told Sam you'd send the quote by Thursday. It's Wednesday.

That last one is the whole point. My own promises, coming back to me before they become a problem.

## For freelancers and small businesses

If you work for yourself, this is where it really earns its place, because your clients are your business.

| Moment | What I ask | What comes back |
|---|---|---|
| A client emails out of the blue | "What's the history with this client?" | Past projects, what we agreed, anything still open |
| Starting a new job | "How did I run the last job like this?" | The project, what went wrong, and the how-tos it used |
| Quiet week | "Who should I follow up with?" | People I haven't spoken to in a while who have something open |
| End of a project | "Close this out with the client." | A summary of what was delivered, and a note on the client for next time |

## Some boundaries I keep

Keeping notes on people deserves a bit of care, so I hold myself to a few rules:

- **It's for remembering commitments, not keeping score.** What I promised, what they asked for, what we decided.
- **Nothing told in confidence goes in.** If someone tells me something private, it stays in my head, not in a system.
- **Contact details sit behind their own key.** Not every AI I connect needs to see people's phone numbers.
- **If it's a business, the people in it have rights over that data.** Treat client records the way you'd want your own treated.

## The small thing that changes everything

I used to think being reliable was about willpower. It turns out it's mostly about having the right reminder at the right moment, and a record good enough that I never have to bluff.

Now when someone asks "did you get a chance to…?", the answer is usually "yes, I sent it Tuesday". And when it isn't, I knew before they asked.
