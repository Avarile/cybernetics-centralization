Every small team has a version of this story. Someone goes on holiday, or leaves, and three days later nobody can remember how the monthly invoicing works. It was never written down. It just lived in their head.

This whole series has been about doing the migration for one person: one memory, any AI, closed loops. The same three ideas work for a team, and the payoff is bigger, because a team forgets in more places at once.

## Open loops, at team scale

Most teams already use a project tool. Tasks get created, assigned and ticked off. But look closely at what it records, and what it doesn't:

- It records **that** a task was done, not **how**, or with which knowledge.
- It rarely records **how it turned out**: did the fix hold? Did the client come back?
- Reviews happen when there's time, which is never, so the same mistake gets made twice by two different people.

The plan-do part is covered. The check-and-improve part is missing. That's an open loop, and every open loop is a lesson the team pays for again.

## Three changes

### 1. One record for the whole team

Two kinds of information, in one place the team owns:

- **What happened:** finished work, decisions, spending, outcomes. The facts.
- **What's happening:** goals, projects, tasks, and the how-tos they rely on. The live work.

Every task links to the knowledge it needed. So when someone asks "how do we do this?", the answer is the same no matter who's asking, or who's away.

### 2. Any AI, with a key per person

The record doesn't live inside any AI company's app. Each person connects whichever AI they already use, with their own key, and that key opens only what they need.

Nobody shares a login. When someone leaves, you cancel their key and nothing else changes. And when a better AI comes out, the team can switch without migrating anything.

### 3. Closed loops, by default

Every project ends with a **check** step before it's done: what was planned, what happened, what we learned. Lessons don't go into a slide deck. They go into the how-to note or the team's templates, so the next person starts from the improved version.

## What a week looks like

With made-up examples from a demo team:

- **Monday:** the weekly report drafts itself from what actually got done, using the team's report template. Someone reads it and corrects two lines, instead of chasing five people for updates.
- **Tuesday:** a new teammate asks their AI "how do we deploy the website?" and gets the team's actual how-to, including the step everyone forgets.
- **Thursday:** a project closes. The retrospective is built from the chain of events already on record: who did what, when, using which knowledge, and how it turned out.
- **Friday:** one lesson from Thursday gets added to the how-to. Next time, nobody has to learn it again.

## Lean on purpose

The part I like most is how little there is to adopt. Most people don't learn a new app. They keep talking to the AI they already use; it just knows more now.

The record is the only genuinely new thing. Good practice spreads through saved recipes (officially "skills"): written instructions for how the team does something, which any AI can follow.

## Where to start

Don't migrate the whole company. Pick one process that hurts.

1. **Choose it.** The weekly report, onboarding a new person, handing over a client.
2. **Write the how-to** the way the best person on the team does it.
3. **Connect one AI** with a key that opens only that part of the record.
4. **Run it for four weeks**, closing every instance with a check step and putting what you learn back into the how-to.

After four weeks you'll have your own evidence, from your own team, to decide whether to do the next process.

## The honest caveats

- **Someone has to own the structure.** Not the content, just the shape: what kinds of entries exist and how they link. Without an owner it drifts back into a pile.
- **What goes in is what comes out.** An AI reading a messy record gives messy answers, faster.
- **Keys need discipline.** One per person, only what's needed, cancelled when people leave.

None of that is new. Good teams have always written things down and looked back. The difference now is that writing it down and looking back are the parts an AI can do most of, and that the knowledge stays when people walk out the door.
