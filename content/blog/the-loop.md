That's the last part of the cyborg: **learning**.

## Why most plans never get better

Most of us are pretty good at planning and doing: to-do lists, calendars, New Year's resolutions. Then we skip the next two steps: **check** (what actually happened) and **act** (what we change because of it).

Looking back properly is hard when you'd have to piece the week together from memory and scattered apps. But for three weeks we've made sure nothing is scattered. Every to-do, fix and note is in one place, with a diary of what got done. For the first time, looking back is *easy*, and that means the loop can finally close.

I run three loops: one weekly, one per project, and one for the system itself.

## Loop 1: the honest weekly review

Remember the review recipe from week two? Time to use it.

> Do my weekly review.

What I get back:

- **What moved**, grouped by goal, where every line comes from something real.
- **What got stuck**, and for how long. The uncomfortable part.
- **What happened that I didn't plan**, straight from the diary.
- **Three suggestions for next week**, which it *asks* me about before changing anything.

Five minutes. And tomorrow's morning brief already knows about it. Week one feeds week two.

## Loop 2: finishing things properly

A small trick that made a big difference: in my system, nothing goes straight from "doing" to "done". There's a step in between, **check it**: did it actually work, and what did I learn? Only then is it done.

> Review is a state, not a wish.

In my real setup there are a few check steps (reviewing, validating, testing). For most people, one is plenty. When I close a project (in the demo, "Fix home Wi-Fi for good"), the AI moves it to "check it" and gives me a short look back: what I planned, what actually happened, how long it took, what went wrong. Then it asks what I should keep doing and what should change. Two minutes, and every project leaves something behind.

## Loop 3: lessons that stick

This is the one that really matters. A lesson you just *think about* is forgotten by Thursday, so every lesson has to land somewhere real:

| If the lesson is about… | It changes… | Example (demo) |
|---|---|---|
| How to do something | A **how-to note** | "Turn off router auto-updates after any reset" |
| How I work | One of **my templates** | The project template gains "check in one week after finishing" |
| The AI getting something wrong | The **recipe** | The "remember this" recipe gets a line fixing what it kept misfiling |

So next time, the system doesn't just remember what happened. It *does things differently*. Mistakes become memory.

## Why this adds up

Look at what we've built in four weeks. My memory feeds the AI. The AI helps me and writes down what happened. The diary feeds my weekly review. The review improves my notes, my templates and my recipes. And round again.

This is the answer to the first post, and the reason the gap grows. It's not that people with a system like this are smarter. It's that every week their system gets a little better, *and keeps* that improvement. Without it, you start every week from the same place. One line stays flat; the other slowly curves up; and the space between them gets bigger every week.

> The gap compounds. So does the advantage.
