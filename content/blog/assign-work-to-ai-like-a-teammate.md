For a long time I used AI like a very fast intern with no memory. Every job started with a long briefing. Every result lived in a chat I'd never find again. And if it got something wrong, the lesson vanished when I closed the tab.

Now I do something that sounds almost old-fashioned. I write the task on a board, assign it, and review it when it's done. The only difference is that sometimes the assignee isn't a person.

## A chat is the wrong shape for work

Chat is great for questions. For work, it's missing everything that makes work manageable:

- **No owner.** Nobody is "on" it once the conversation ends.
- **No status.** Is it started? Stuck? Done? Waiting for me?
- **No history.** What was done, by whom, based on what?
- **No review.** The result just appears, and either you notice the mistake or you don't.

Teams solved this decades ago with a simple idea: a board of tasks, each with an owner and a status. It turns out that works for AI too.

## The board, with one twist

My project board is the classic kind. Goals at the top, projects under each goal, tasks under each project, and every task moves through statuses: to do, doing, check it, done.

The twist is that a task can be assigned to an agent. "Agent" is the technical word for an AI that can take steps on its own, like reading notes and updating tasks, rather than just replying. On my board, agents and people show up in the same assignee list.

## What happens when I assign a task

Here's a made-up example from my demo notebook. Under the project "Sort out tax return" there's a task: "Gather everything I need for the tax return". I assign it to an agent.

1. **It reads the task**, and the project and goal it belongs to.
2. **It reads the notes linked to it.** In this case "Tax deductions I can claim", a how-to I wrote last year.
3. **It looks for what's missing**, using the money section to see which categories of receipts I'll need.
4. **It writes its result on the task**: a checklist of documents, with a line saying which notes it relied on.
5. **It moves the task to "check it".** Never to "done".

Then it's my turn. I read the checklist, fix one line, and move it to done. Or I send it back with a comment, just as I would with a person.

## Four rules I give every agent

These live in a saved recipe (officially a "skill") that every agent follows:

1. **Only work on what's assigned to you.** No freelancing across the board.
2. **Read the linked notes first.** If something you need isn't there, say so instead of guessing.
3. **Leave a comment saying what you did and what you relied on.** That's the receipt.
4. **Never mark anything done.** Move it to "check it" and let a person close it.

> Review is a state, not a wish.

## Why this works better than chatting

- **The work outlives the conversation.** The result sits on the task, linked to the project, where I'll find it next month.
- **I can see the reasoning.** Every result says which notes it was based on, so I can tell a good answer from a lucky one.
- **Mistakes turn into improvements.** When I correct an agent, the fix goes into the how-to note or the recipe, so the next task starts from the corrected version. That's the closed loop.
- **People and agents share one board.** I can see at a glance what's waiting on me, what's waiting on someone else, and what an agent is chewing on.

## What I still do myself

Handing work to agents isn't the same as handing over judgement. I still:

- decide what matters and what goes on the board,
- review everything before it's done,
- handle anything that moves money, and anything said to another person in my name.

## Try it with one task

You don't need a fancy setup to test the idea. Pick one recurring job you dread, write it as a task with a clear "done looks like…", link the note that explains how you do it, and let an AI take the first pass. Then review it like you'd review a colleague.

If the result is useful, you've just learned what a good brief looks like. If it isn't, you've learned what your note was missing. Either way, the next attempt is better. That's the whole idea.
